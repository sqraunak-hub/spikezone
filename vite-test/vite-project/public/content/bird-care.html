Deterring and Feeding Are Not Opposites

People sometimes ask how a company selling bird spikes also sells bird feeders. It is the same principle applied twice.

Nothing we make harms a bird. Spikes remove the footing from a parapet or an AC unit so the bird chooses somewhere else — and if there is food and water twenty feet away in the garden, it has somewhere better to choose. That is a far more durable outcome than trying to make an entire neighbourhood inhospitable.

The conflict people imagine only exists if the goal is to get rid of birds. It is not. The goal is to keep them off the surfaces where they cause damage, mess and disease risk, and that is fully compatible with wanting them around.

The Pigeon Problem With Feeding

Feeding pigeons on a balcony creates the problem

This has to be said plainly, because it is the single commonest cause of the complaints described everywhere else on this site.

Scattering grain on a balcony, window sill or terrace does not feed “birds” in general. It feeds pigeons, because pigeons are the species that arrives in numbers, displaces everything smaller, and then nests on the building that fed it. Within a season you have a resident flock, droppings on every ledge below, and neighbours who did not agree to any of it.

A feeder is a different thing from scattered grain. It holds a measured amount off the ground, in one place, and — the important part — its ports and perches are small. A sparrow, munia or silverbill can use it. A pigeon cannot get at it.

So the honest guidance is: feed with a feeder, site it away from the building, and stop scattering grain on the ledges.

Why This Matters More Than It Used To

The house sparrow — the bird most Indians grew up with in every courtyard — has declined sharply in Indian cities over the past few decades. The usual explanations are the same everywhere: sealed modern buildings with no nesting cavities, paved-over ground, fewer insects for chicks, and less open water.

Two of those you can do something about from a balcony. Water is the bigger one, and it is the one people underestimate:

Doing It Without Creating a Nuisance

A feeder is a small thing that affects your neighbours, so a few habits keep it welcome:

  • Site it away from the building. A tree, a pole, a garden hook — not the parapet, not the AC unit, not the sill.
  • Use the tray. Spilled grain on the ground below is what attracts rats and pigeons, and it is what neighbours actually object to.
  • Keep it clean. A dirty feeder spreads disease between birds — it works against the thing you are trying to do.
  • Change water daily. Standing water is a mosquito issue in Indian cities, and that matters more than the birds do.
  • Small quantities, refilled often, rather than a large fill that sits and spoils.
  • Do not feed pigeons. Everything above stops working if you do.

Frequently Asked Questions

You sell bird spikes. Why do you also sell feeders?

Because both come from the same position: birds belong in the right places. Spikes keep them off surfaces where they cause damage; feeders give them somewhere better to be. Nothing we make is designed to harm a bird.

Will a feeder attract pigeons to my flat?

Not this kind. The ports and perches are sized for small birds, so sparrows, munias and silverbills can use it and pigeons cannot. What attracts pigeons is loose grain scattered on a balcony or terrace — that is the practice to stop.

Food or water — which should I start with?

Water, especially between March and June. Natural water disappears from Indian cities in summer while food is generally still findable, so a refilled water tray is the higher-value thing.

Where should I hang it?

Away from the building itself — a tree, a hook, a stand — in shade if you can, with some cover nearby so birds feel safe approaching. Not on a ledge, sill or parapet.

Is feeding birds legal?

Feeding garden birds is entirely legal. Some housing societies and municipal areas restrict feeding pigeons specifically, because of the droppings and health complaints that follow. A small-bird feeder sited in a garden is a different matter from scattering grain in a common area.

Do you supply to schools and societies?

Yes, in bulk. Feeders are a common choice for school nature programmes and society garden committees, and they are quoted per unit.

Somewhere Better to Be

If you are dealing with birds on your building, the surfaces are only half of it. Give them water and a feeder away from the structure and the problem gets easier, not harder.
