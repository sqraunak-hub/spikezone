The projecting body of a window AC gives a flat shelf at exactly the height birds like, with the window reveal sheltering it on both sides and the sunshade often covering it from above. It is a three-sided alcove with a floor.

Two things then make it better than any other spot on the building: the gap around the unit, and the water.

The Two Things Only a Window AC Has

The Gap Leads Inside

Nesting material ends up in your window recess

A window AC is rarely a perfect fit in the opening. There is almost always a gap around the casing, filled with foam, wood packing or nothing at all. Birds work into that gap, and unlike a split unit — where the cavity is against a solid wall — this one opens back toward the room.

That is how nesting material, feathers and mites end up on the inside sill. Sealing the gap properly is half the job here, and it is the half that most people skip because spikes on the outside feel like the whole answer.

The Drip is a Water Source

A window AC sheds condensate through the summer, which is when open water is hardest for birds to find in a city. A unit that drips onto its own casing or into a tray below is supplying drinking water on demand, and birds will use a spot with water over an identical spot without it.

Where the drip collects rather than falling clear — a blocked drain, a sagging tray, a ledge below — fixing the drainage removes part of the attraction before anything is fitted.

Do not block the rear grille

Like any air conditioner, a window unit sheds heat by moving air through the back and sides. Spikes belong on the top and on the support frame — never across the intake or discharge grilles, and never wrapped in net. If it cannot breathe, it works harder.

This One You Can Usually Do Yourself

Unlike a split unit on a high wall bracket, a window AC is at window height and reachable from inside. That makes it one of the few surfaces on this site where no external access is needed.

  1. Switch the unit off at the plug or isolator.
  2. Check for an active nest first. Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 — if there are eggs or chicks in the gap or on the frame, wait until they fledge before clearing.
  3. Clear the top and the gap, gloves and mask on, dampening droppings before removing them.
  4. Seal the gap around the casing with proper packing rather than cloth or newspaper, which birds pull straight back out.
  5. Check the drain runs clear and drips away from the unit rather than pooling on it.
  6. Fit spikes on the top and the support frame, keeping clear of the grilles.
  7. Do the sunshade above at the same time, or they move up a foot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do birds nest around window AC units?

Three things together: a flat shelf on the projecting body, a sheltered alcove formed by the window reveal, and water from the condensate drip through the summer. Very little else on a building offers all three.

Nesting material keeps appearing inside my room. Why?

Because the gap around the casing opens back toward the room. Unlike a split unit, where the cavity is against a solid wall, a window AC's gap is a route inside. Sealing it properly is half the fix.

Can I put a net over my window AC?

No. The unit needs airflow through the back and sides. Use spikes on the top and the support frame, keep the grilles clear, and seal the gap around the casing.

Will spikes affect the cooling?

Not if they are on the top panel and the support frame and clear of the intake and discharge grilles. What does affect cooling is a nest packed into the casing, which is what this prevents.

Can I fit this myself?

Usually yes, and this is one of the few surfaces where that is true — a window AC is reachable from inside. Switch the unit off first, and check there is no active nest before you clear anything.

What about the water dripping from the unit?

That drip is part of the attraction in summer. If it pools on the casing or on a ledge below rather than falling clear, clearing the drain removes some of the appeal before anything is fitted.
