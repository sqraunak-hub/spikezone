Decorative mouldings are the projecting bands, string courses, brackets, medallions and carved profiles that give a building its character — on temples, havelis, colonial facades, hotel frontages, institutional buildings and any home with ornamental plasterwork.

Birds like them for the obvious reason: an ornamental profile provides dozens of small footholds and pockets on an otherwise flat wall. What is unusual about this surface is not why they land there. It is what happens to the moulding afterwards.

The Droppings Are Eating the Detail

This damage is permanent

Bird droppings are acidic. Left on lime plaster, sandstone, marble or painted moulding they etch the surface, and the finer the carving the faster the detail is lost. On stone and traditional plaster the loss is not reversible — a cleaned moulding is a clean damaged moulding.

There is a structural side too. Nesting material packed into a decorative profile holds water against the substrate, which on lime plaster and old masonry causes spalling and loosens the moulding from the wall. On older buildings, pieces do eventually come down.

Repeated aggressive cleaning is its own hazard: pressure washing carved plaster to remove droppings takes off more material than the droppings did.

“It Will Ruin the Look”

This is the real reason decorative mouldings go untreated, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a sales one.

Yes — a heavy, wide, brightly finished row fitted along the front face of an ornamental band will look worse than the birds. Nobody should accept that on a facade they care about. But that is a specification failure, not something inherent to the product.

The Profile Is Not Flat

A ledge is a shelf; a moulding is a shape. That is a fitting problem, and it is what makes the work slower here than the metres would suggest.

  • Find the flat. Almost every moulding has one usable horizontal somewhere in the profile — the top of a string course, the fillet above a curve. That is where the row goes, not on the ornament.
  • Break the run at every feature. Brackets, medallions and returns interrupt the band; the material is cut around them rather than bridged over.
  • Curved sections take segments. A rigid base will not follow an ogee or a rounded profile — the same principle as curved surfaces.
  • Do not miss the small pockets. Bracket tops and the hollow above a medallion are often exactly where the nest is, and they are missed because they are not part of the run.
  • Fix to the substrate, not the ornament, wherever the profile allows a choice.

Protected and heritage buildings

If the building is a protected monument or listed under a state or ASI heritage designation, written consent is normally needed before anything is attached — including bird deterrents. Obtain it first. It also usually results in a better specification, because the conservation officer will insist on reversibility, which is what you wanted anyway.

Sequence

  1. Get consent if the building is protected. Before survey, not after.
  2. Check for nests in the pockets and behind brackets. Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972.
  3. Photograph from street level and record the horizontal in the profile — that decides the width and the setback.
  4. Clean gently. On lime plaster and soft stone, aggressive washing does more harm than the droppings. Take advice on the method.
  5. Repair before protecting. Fitting over already-spalled plaster fixes the deterrent to something that is coming off the wall.
  6. Fit set back from the front edge, cut around every feature.
  7. Include the pockets — bracket tops and medallion hollows.
  8. Look at it from the road afterwards. That is the test that matters on this surface.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will spikes spoil the look of my decorative moulding?

Not if they are specified for it. The polycarbonate is transparent, so there is no colour to sit against the stone, and a narrow section set back from the front edge is not picked out at street level. What looks bad is a wide, opaque row fitted along the front face — which is a specification choice, not something inherent to the product.

Do bird droppings really damage stone and plaster?

Yes. They are acidic and etch lime plaster, sandstone, marble and paintwork. On carved work the fine detail goes first, and that loss is permanent — cleaning removes the stain, not the erosion.

Can this be done on a heritage building?

Usually, but written consent is normally required first if the building is protected or listed. Ask before the survey. Conservation approval tends to require a reversible fixing, which is the right specification regardless.

Will the fixings damage the carving?

They should not. On ornamental work the row is fixed to the flat part of the profile or the substrate, not through the ornament, and on protected buildings a removable method is used.

The moulding is curved. Will a straight strip fit?

Not in one length. Curved profiles are followed in short segments so the base stays in contact — a rigid strip touches at the ends and lifts in the middle.

Can it be removed later?

Yes — it lifts off and re-fixes in about a minute with no special tools. On heritage and protected buildings that reversibility is usually the deciding point, so say at quotation stage that it matters and the fixing method is chosen to match.

Our plasterwork is already crumbling. Should we fit anyway?

Repair first. Anything fitted to spalled plaster is attached to material that is already leaving the wall — and the birds are part of why it is spalling.

Can you quote from photographs?

Yes. Send one from street level and one close-up of the profile, plus the total run. The close-up tells us where the usable horizontal is; the street shot tells us how discreet it has to be.

Protecting Decorative Stonework or Plaster?

Send a close-up of the moulding profile and a shot from the road, with the run in metres. If it is a heritage or protected building, say so — it changes both the fixing method and the approvals we will ask you to obtain first.
