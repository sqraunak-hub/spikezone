Bird Problems in Warehouses & Godowns

In a warehouse the birds are rarely the problem people call about. What they call about is droppings on cartons, on racking, on a food-grade floor, and an auditor who has written it up. The birds are upstairs in the trusses; the consequence is downstairs on the stock.

  • The buyer is usually the QA or facilities manager, working to a closure date.
  • Specify mesh from the smallest bird present — a sparrow inside a food warehouse is the same finding as a pigeon.
  • Close the openings before netting the roof. One is a fraction of the cost of the other.
  • Nothing may fall into stock — that is a bigger incident than the birds.

A warehouse is close to ideal habitat: a huge covered volume, horizontal steel to perch on every few metres, no predators, no weather, and doors that stand open for hours. Once birds are established in the roof structure, everything below is downwind of them.

Unlike a balcony, you cannot protect the surfaces one at a time — there are hundreds. Warehouse bird control is about exclusion: stopping entry, and separating the roof void from the floor.

How Birds Get In — and It Is Always One of These

Before specifying anything, find the entry route. Netting the roof void while a ventilator stands open achieves very little.

  • Roller shutters left open during loading. The commonest route by a distance, and the hardest to change because it is operational rather than physical.
  • Ridge vents and turbo ventilators without mesh.
  • Gaps at the eaves where the sheet meets the wall — a continuous opening running the length of the building.
  • Damaged or displaced roof sheets, and missing filler pieces at the corrugation profile.
  • Louvres and exhaust openings in the gable ends.
  • Service penetrations where pipes and cables pass through the wall.

Find the route before you buy anything

Stand inside with the lights off in daytime. Every gap in the envelope shows as a line of daylight. That five-minute exercise will tell you more about your bird problem than any amount of watching them fly.

The Two Strategies, and When Each Applies

Strategy 1 — Exclude at the Envelope

Close the entry routes: mesh the ventilators, fill the eaves gap, net the gable louvres, fix the damaged sheets. This is the cheapest per square metre and the most durable answer, and it is the right one where the building envelope is basically sound and the doors can be managed.

Strategy 2 — Separate the Roof Void from the Floor

Net horizontally below the truss line, across the full span. Birds may still enter the roof void, but nothing they do up there reaches the stock below. This is the answer where shutters must stand open through the shift and sealing the envelope is not realistic.

Most warehouses need both

Seal what can be sealed, and net horizontally over the area that actually matters — usually the racking and the packing bays rather than the whole footprint. Protecting the zone that carries the product costs a fraction of protecting the building and satisfies the same audit finding.

Clearing an Established Roost

  1. Survey with the lights off in daytime and mark every daylight gap.
  2. Check for active nests. Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 — wait for chicks to fledge before clearing.
  3. Cover or move stock before any work at height starts.
  4. Clean at height with the area below cordoned. Dampen droppings rather than sweeping dry; the dust is the part worth avoiding.
  5. Close the envelope — mesh, filler pieces, sheet repairs.
  6. Install the exclusion while the roost is empty, not while it is in use.
  7. Clear the gutters and downpipes of nesting debris.
  8. Add it to the monthly walk. A new gap is cheap to close and expensive to ignore.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I stop birds getting into a warehouse?

Find the entry route first — stand inside with the lights off in daytime and every gap shows as daylight. Then close the ventilators, eaves gaps and louvres with mesh or net. Where shutters must stay open, net horizontally below the trusses instead.

Is bird netting or are spikes better for a warehouse?

Netting, at this scale. There are hundreds of perchable surfaces in a roof structure and spiking them individually is not practical. Spikes are for the specific items — girder flanges, cable trays, light fittings, racking tops.

Do I have to net the whole warehouse?

Usually not. Netting horizontally over the zone that carries product — racking and packing bays — addresses the contamination and the audit finding at a fraction of the cost of the full footprint.

Will bird control help with a food safety audit?

Bird activity is a standard audit line item and one of the easiest for an auditor to evidence. Physical exclusion plus a documented inspection routine is what auditors generally want to see. We supply the products; the documentation is yours to keep.

There are already nests in the trusses. Can we clear them?

Not while there are eggs or chicks. Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972. Wait for the young to fledge, then clear and exclude immediately, while the roost is empty.

Can netting be installed while the warehouse is operating?

Usually in phases, bay by bay, with the area below cordoned. It is worth planning around stock movement rather than working over live racking.

What quantities do you supply?

Whatever the span needs. Warehouse orders run to thousands of square metres of net and hundreds of metres of spike, and that is the scale we manufacture for.
