In a house you can solve a bird problem yourself. In a flat you often cannot — because the parapet is common property, the droppings come from the floor above, and the balcony next door is untouched. Knowing which of those you are dealing with saves a lot of wasted effort.

What One Flat Can Fix Alone

These are inside your own boundary. No permission, no committee, and most are reachable without external access.

  • Window AC unit — reachable from inside, and one of the few genuinely DIY jobs.
  • Your own window sills
  • Balcony railing
  • The balcony itself, including netting the opening — though many societies require approval for netting because it changes the elevation.
  • Your split AC outdoor unit, if it is on your own wall and reachable.

What Needs the Society

The problem above you is not yours to fix

This is the single most common frustration in flats. You spike your own sill, and the streaking continues — because it is coming from the sill two floors up. Nothing you do to your own flat stops that.

Common areas need a common decision: the terrace parapet, the roof and water tank, the building elevation, stilt parking, and the lift machine room. All of them are where the population actually lives.

Getting It Approved: What Actually Works

Committees say no to vague requests and yes to specific ones. If you are the person raising it, the following gets it through faster than anything else.

  1. Photograph the source, not your own mess. A picture of the terrace parapet with twenty pigeons on it is an argument. A picture of your dirty balcony is a complaint.
  2. Lead with the water tank. Of everything on a society roof, droppings around the overhead tank is the point that gets attention, and it is a small quantity to fix.
  3. Bring a number. Parapet perimeter × coping width tells the committee what it costs. "We should do something about the pigeons" does not.
  4. Tie it to the painting cycle. If the building is due for repainting, the access is already being paid for. Doing both together is dramatically cheaper and it is the easiest yes a committee will give.
  5. Propose a phase. One elevation, or just the terrace. A smaller first step gets approved where a whole-building quote stalls.

Before You Clear Anything

Active nests

Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972. If there are eggs or chicks, wait until the young have fledged — commonly four to six weeks from hatching — then clear and protect the site straight away, before the pair returns.

It is also the practical choice. A displaced pair rebuilds within days, usually a metre away, and you have gained nothing.

Frequently Asked Questions

The droppings come from the flat above. What can I do?

Protecting your own sill stops birds landing on yours, but what lands above still runs down. On a shared facade this genuinely needs a building-level decision, which is why these orders usually go through the committee.

Do I need society permission?

For spikes on your own sill or AC unit, generally not. For balcony netting, usually yes — it changes the appearance of the elevation and most societies have a view on it. For anything on the terrace or facade, always.

Can I install it myself?

Window AC units, window sills and railings are usually within reach and are the realistic DIY jobs. Anything needing external access at height is not.

Where should a society start?

The water tank rim, then the terrace parapet. Small quantity, and it removes the main roost that feeds the rest of the building.

Is it cheaper to do it with the painting?

Considerably. Access is the expensive part of any work at height, and if scaffold or a cradle is already up for painting, the marginal cost is close to material only.

Do you supply for one flat?

Yes. Small quantities are supplied as readily as bulk, and most society orders start as one flat.
