Birds use trusses because a truss offers exactly what a cliff ledge offers: a flat horizontal member to stand on, a vertical behind it, and cover overhead from the roof sheet. The node points — where diagonal members meet the bottom chord — are the prize. They form a small sheltered platform with support on three sides, and that is where the nests go.

Everything below then receives what happens up there: stock, machinery, floors, people.

Why We Do Not Recommend Spiking a Truss

We sell spikes. This is not the surface for them.

A single truss has a bottom chord, a top chord, and a dozen or more diagonal members, with a node point every two to three metres. A shed has a truss every four or five metres. Protecting all of that surface individually means hundreds of small pieces, each one fixed at height, at awkward angles, on galvanised steel that adhesive does not like.

Even done perfectly it leaves the purlins, and the birds move to those. The cost of the access alone — scaffold or boom, for days — usually exceeds the cost of netting the entire span once.

There are two exceptions, and they are worth knowing:

  • A short run over one critical area. If one truss sits directly above a packing bench or a panel and the rest of the shed is not in use, spiking that member is sensible and cheap.
  • Wide flat girder flanges rather than a lattice truss. A plate girder has a broad flat flange, which is a genuine ledge and takes spikes well — see girders.

What Netting Below the Truss Line Actually Does

A horizontal net stretched below the bottom chord separates the roof void from everything under it. Birds may still get into the void. Nothing they do there reaches the floor.

You do not have to net the whole shed

Net the bays that carry product or people — racking, packing, the line, the inspection area. A partial installation over the zone that matters costs a fraction of the full footprint and removes the same contamination risk. This is the single biggest saving available on a truss job and it is rarely suggested.

Close the Entry First, Whichever You Choose

Neither spikes nor net achieve much while the birds are still flying in freely. Before specifying anything, find the route in.

  • Ridge vents and turbo ventilators without mesh
  • The continuous gap at the eaves where sheet meets wall
  • Missing filler pieces at the corrugation profile
  • Gable-end louvres
  • Damaged or displaced roof sheets
  • Roller shutters standing open through the shift

The lights-off test

Stand inside in daytime with the lights off. Every gap in the envelope shows as a line of daylight. Five minutes, no equipment, and it tells you more than watching the birds for a week.

Doing the Work

  1. Survey with the lights off and mark every entry point.
  2. Check for active nests. Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 — wait for chicks to fledge before clearing.
  3. Decide the zone. Full span or the bays that matter. This is the decision that sets the budget.
  4. Book the access once. Scaffold or boom is the expensive part, so clean, close the envelope and install in the same visit rather than three.
  5. Clear the roof void with the floor below cordoned. Dampen droppings rather than sweeping dry.
  6. Install while the roost is empty.
  7. Clear the gutters of nesting debris before the monsoon.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can bird spikes be fitted on roof trusses?

Physically yes, practically no. A truss has hundreds of members and node points, all at height and at angles, and even a complete job leaves the purlins. The access cost alone usually exceeds netting the span once.

What is the best way to stop birds nesting in trusses?

Close the entry points in the building envelope, then net horizontally below the truss line so the roof void is separated from the floor. Spikes are for the specific exceptions, not the structure.

Do I have to net the entire shed?

No, and this is where most of the saving is. Netting only the bays over racking, packing or the production line removes the contamination risk at a fraction of the full-footprint cost.

Why do birds always nest at the same points on a truss?

Node points — where the diagonals meet the bottom chord — give shelter on three sides and a flat base. It is the closest thing in a steel building to a natural ledge, which is why every truss in the shed is used at the same spots.

Will netting interfere with sprinklers or lighting?

It has to be planned around them. Send photographs showing the services below the truss line and the net can be set at a level that clears them.

There are nests up there now. When can we clear them?

Not while there are eggs or chicks — most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972. Plan the access for after they fledge and install immediately, while the structure is empty.

How is a truss job quoted?

By the square metres of net for the area being covered, plus any spikes for the exceptions. Send the bay dimensions and how many bays need protecting.
