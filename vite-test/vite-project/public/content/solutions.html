Start With the Surface, Not the Product

Most bird control that fails, fails because the wrong product was fitted to the surface. Spikes stop a bird landing on an edge. Netting stops a bird entering a space. Fitting spikes to an open balcony, or netting to a narrow ledge, wastes both.

Reading the Droppings

Before choosing anything, work out what the birds are actually doing. Almost every wrong purchase on this site comes from treating a nesting problem as a perching one. You can usually tell without seeing a single bird, from the pattern the droppings leave.

PatternBehaviourWhat it means for the fix
An even scatter along a line Perching. Birds landing, sitting, moving on. The easiest case. A row along the landing surface is usually enough.
One heavy concentrated pile Nesting. A resident pair using one spot. Harder, and time-sensitive. Nothing can be sealed while there are chicks.
Heavy over a wide area, worst in the morning Roosting. A group sleeping overhead. Usually structural — trusses, girders, high steel. Often netting below.
Streaking down a wall face Long-term use of something above. Look higher than the mess. The source is often a floor or two up.
Twigs, straw or feathers present Nesting, confirmed. Check for eggs or chicks before touching anything.

If you photograph nothing else, photograph where the droppings are heaviest. It tells us more than a description of the building does.

Before You Fit Anything: the Legal Position

Active nests are protected

Most wild birds in India are protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972. In practice this means an active nest — one with eggs or chicks in it — must not be disturbed, destroyed or sealed over, whatever it is costing you.

This is not a formality on enclosed surfaces. Sealing a pelmet, a cavity or a recess with young inside traps them, which is both illegal and something you would then be living with.

The practical answer is timing. Wait until the young have fledged, then clear and fit within the same week. A pair returns to the same site year after year, so that short window between one brood leaving and the next season starting is the entire opportunity. Miss it and you wait another cycle.

Open perching surfaces have no such restriction — those can be done whenever.

What Does Not Work

Nearly every customer has tried something before calling. It is worth being direct about why the common remedies fail, because it explains what a deterrent has to actually do.

MethodWhy it fails
Hanging CDs, foil strips, reflective tapeWorks for a few days on novelty alone. Birds habituate fast, and the strips end up as litter on the neighbour’s balcony.
Plastic owls and hawk modelsStatic and silent. Birds learn within a week that it never moves, and often perch on it.
Ultrasonic devicesPigeons hear roughly the same range humans do. The frequencies marketed as inaudible to people are largely inaudible to the birds too.
Gels and sticky pastesThey work by trapping feet, which is cruel, and they collect dust until they stop being sticky. They also stain masonry permanently.
Loose wire or string across a ledgeWrong tension and spacing, and it works loose. On an exposed edge the pieces fall on whatever is below.
Cleaning more oftenRemoves the evidence, not the reason. A cleaned ledge is a fresh ledge.
Blocking one spot onlyBirds move a few feet. Unless the whole surface is treated, you have relocated the problem, not solved it.

Physical exclusion is the only approach that keeps working after the birds have got used to it — a surface that cannot be stood on, or an opening that cannot be entered.

One Cause Worth Checking Before You Buy Anything

Is somebody feeding them?

If grain is being scattered on a terrace, a balcony or in a society common area, no amount of hardware holds. Feeding concentrates pigeons in one place, and pigeons that are fed on a building nest on that building. You can protect every ledge on the block and the pressure simply moves to the ones you have not done yet.

In housing societies this is usually well-meant, often religious, and almost never discussed — which is why deterrents get blamed for failing. It is a committee conversation, and it needs to happen alongside the physical work rather than after it.

Feeding birds is not the problem in itself. Feeding pigeons, on the structure, with loose grain, is. A proper feeder sited away from the building has small ports that pigeons cannot use at all — see bird care and water feeders, which is the version of this that helps rather than backfires.

Doing a Whole Building: Work Top Down

If the problem is the building rather than one balcony, the order matters more than the products. Birds arrive at the highest, outermost line and work inward and downward from there, so protection follows the same path:

  1. Rooftop and roof edge — the approach line and the water tank area.
  2. Parapet — the longest continuous run on most buildings.
  3. Cornices and mouldings — the sheltered corners where nesting actually happens.
  4. Facade bands and ledges, floor by floor.
  5. Balconies, sills and AC units — the occupant-level surfaces.
  6. Porch and pillars at ground level.

The common mistake is starting at step five, because that is where the complaints come from. Protecting balconies while the roof edge and parapet stay open does not reduce the population on the building — it redistributes it, and the next complaint comes from a different flat.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know which surface is actually the problem?

Follow the droppings. A line of droppings along the ground means the birds are sitting on a horizontal edge directly above it. A scattered patch usually means they are flying through rather than perching.

Should I start from the surface or the product?

The surface, always. Its width and shape decide the product, the row count and the fixing method. Choosing a product first is how people end up with a wide strip overhanging a three-inch sill.

What if my building has several problem areas?

Treat them together and work top down — parapet first, then chajjas and sills, then railings and AC units. Doing one surface at a time just moves the birds to the next untreated edge.

How is any of this priced?

On running length — the total metres of edge to be treated — plus fixings. Surface width matters too, because anything over about four inches needs two rows.

Are spikes and nets legal to fit?

Yes. Both are passive, non-lethal deterrents and are permitted on residential and commercial buildings across India. Netting usually needs society approval because it changes the elevation; spikes usually do not.

Which surfaces are DIY and which are not?

Balconies, sills and AC units are straightforward DIY. Parapet edges, trusses, domes and anything at height need proper access and usually a contractor.

How long before the birds actually go?

Two to three weeks for an established roost. They will keep testing the spot before giving up, and removing the treatment early is the one thing that guarantees they come back.

What if I still cannot identify the surface?

Send us photographs of where the droppings are and where you have seen the birds sitting. We will tell you which surface is the problem and what it needs.
