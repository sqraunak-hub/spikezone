Port Size Decides Which Birds Come

This is the whole design, not a detail

An open dish of grain feeds whoever is biggest and pushiest — which in an Indian city means pigeons and crows. Everything smaller waits, gives up, and stops coming.

A tube feeder with small ports and short perches physically inverts that. A sparrow, munia, silverbill or white-eye can sit on the perch and reach in. A pigeon cannot balance on the perch or get its head into the port, and a crow has no chance at all.

So the feeder is not just a container. It is a filter, and it is the reason a feeder does not create the balcony problems that scattering grain does — see balcony bird problems for what the scattering approach leads to.

Practically, expect house sparrows, munias and silverbills first. Bulbuls and mynas may use the tray. Parakeets will try if the perch takes their weight.

Where to Hang It

Placement decides whether it gets used at all. Birds are cautious about open, exposed feeding spots, and they will not use one that feels unsafe no matter what is in it.

The Tray Is the Part That Keeps the Peace

The base tray looks like a minor feature and is usually the difference between a feeder your neighbours ignore and one they complain about.

  • It catches spill. Birds are messy eaters; without a tray, a share of every fill ends up on the ground.
  • Grain on the ground is what draws rats and pigeons — not the feeder itself. That is almost always the real complaint.
  • It gives smaller birds a second place to stand, so more can feed at once.
  • It needs drainage. A tray that holds rainwater turns grain to paste and, left standing, becomes a mosquito site.

Sweep or rinse the tray when you refill. It takes a few seconds and it is most of the maintenance this product needs.

Getting It Used

  1. Expect a wait. A new feeder often takes one to three weeks to be found. This is normal and is not a sign it is in the wrong place.
  2. Start with a small fill. If it is not being used, a large fill just spoils.
  3. Do not keep moving it. Birds learn locations; shifting it every few days restarts the clock each time.
  4. Water helps it get found. A water feeder nearby brings birds into the area, and they discover the food from there.
  5. Keep it consistent once it is in use. Birds build it into a daily route, and in peak summer or a cold spell that matters.
  6. Clean it regularly. Dismantle, rinse, dry. A dirty feeder spreads disease between the birds you are trying to help.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before birds start using it?

Usually one to three weeks. They have to find it and decide it is safe. Keep the fill small until it is being used, and do not move it while you wait.

Will pigeons take it over?

No. The ports and perches are sized for small birds — a pigeon cannot balance there or reach into the port. That is deliberate, and it is why a feeder behaves very differently from grain scattered on a terrace.

How high should it hang?

About 1.5 to 2 metres. Above cat height, and still within reach for refilling and cleaning without a ladder.

Can I hang it on my balcony?

You can, but hang it clear of the railing and the AC unit rather than on them, keep the tray clean, and accept that it may take longer to be found than a garden position. If you are already dealing with birds on the balcony structure, deal with that first.

Birds keep hitting my window since I put it up.

That is a distance problem. Either move it very close to the glass — under about a metre, so a startled bird cannot build up speed — or well beyond three metres. The middle distance is where strikes happen.

How often should I clean it?

Every refill for the tray, and a full dismantle and rinse periodically — more often in monsoon. A feeder is a place where many birds touch the same surface, so hygiene is part of the point of it.

Do you supply in bulk?

Yes — schools, societies and corporate green initiatives order them by the dozen.
